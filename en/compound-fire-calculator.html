Two numbers decide everything: what you spend, and what you withdraw

Financial independence is a division problem. Take the annual spending you want to fund and divide it by a withdrawal rate. At 4% that is 25 times spending: $40,000 a year needs $1,000,000. At 3.5% it is 28.6 times, at 3% it is 33.3 times. The withdrawal rate is a risk choice, not a law — lower rates buy a longer retirement and a bigger target.

Compounding then decides how long the accumulation takes. With $40,000 invested, $1,500 a month and a 7% return, the nominal balance passes $1,000,000 in about 22 years. Raise the contribution to $2,500 a month and it takes about 17. Double the starting balance and you save roughly two years. What moves the date most, though, is the gap between income and spending — the savings rate — because it changes both the contributions and the target at once.

Why this calculator works in today's dollars

A nominal projection is misleading: $1,000,000 in 22 years buys far less than $1,000,000 today, while spending rises with inflation. So the model subtracts inflation from the return (real return ≈ (1 + return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1) and holds the spending target at today's level. At 7% return and 2.8% inflation the real return is 4.09%, and the projection is a purchasing-power comparison rather than a bigger-looking number. Nominal balance is shown separately, labelled, for anyone who wants the statement value.

Coast FIRE is the quieter milestone

Coast FIRE is the amount you need today so that compound growth alone reaches the FIRE number by your target age, with no further contributions. Discount the FIRE number back over the remaining years at the real return and you have it. Past that point, stopping contributions does not delay retirement as long as the money stays invested — which is why some people cut hours or take lower-paying work long before they retire fully.

What this model leaves out

  • Taxes. Contributions are treated as after-tax, and withdrawal taxes (capital gains, dividend tax, retirement account rules) are not modelled. In accounts taxed on the way out, the target is larger.
  • Fees and fund costs. A 0.5% expense ratio is a 0.5% drag on the return you enter, not a separate line.
  • Sequence risk. A bad first decade of retirement can force selling assets at lows; average returns hide that.
  • Variable spending. Real spending usually falls in the first years of retirement and rises later with health costs.
  • Contribution timing. Contributions are added at month end, so results are marginally conservative compared with payroll investing.

Frequently asked

What is a FIRE number?
The portfolio that funds your annual spending at a chosen withdrawal rate. At 4%, it is 25 times annual spending: $40,000 of spending needs $1,000,000.
Is the 4% rule reliable?
It comes from US historical sequences over 30-year retirements. Longer retirements, high starting valuations, inflation shocks and fees can make it too optimistic — which is why the withdrawal rate is an input here, with 3.5% and 3% shown as alternatives.
Why use a real return instead of the nominal one?
Because your spending target rises with inflation. Comparing a nominal balance to today's spending flatters progress. Keeping both sides in today's dollars gives a number you can act on.
What is Coast FIRE?
The amount needed today so that compound growth alone reaches the FIRE number by your target retirement age. Beyond it, you only need to cover current expenses.
Should I count a paid-off home in the FIRE number?
Count the spending it removes, not its market value. A paid-off home lowers annual housing cost, which lowers the FIRE number. Adding its value to your portfolio double counts unless you plan to sell.
Does a higher return always shorten the date?
In the model, yes; in reality, higher expected returns come with more volatility, and volatility at the wrong moment hurts. Use a lower return for planning and check the result still works.
