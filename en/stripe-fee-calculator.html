The fee is two numbers, and only one of them scales

A card processor charges a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per successful charge. Stripe's US domestic online card rate is 2.9% + $0.30; PayPal's Checkout button is 3.49% + $0.49. On a $100 sale Stripe takes $3.20 and PayPal takes $3.98. The gap looks small until you multiply it by a thousand orders, and it behaves completely differently at a $5 price point: the fixed fee alone is 6% of a $5 sale on Stripe and 9.8% on PayPal.

That is why the advertised rate is not the rate you pay. The effective rate is total fee divided by the sale. At 2.9% + $0.30, a $5 charge carries a 32.9% effective rate and a $500 charge carries 2.96%. Low-ticket businesses — digital downloads, coffee, in-app tips — feel the fixed fee far more than the percentage.

What adds to the baseline

  • International cards: +1.5% on Stripe and +1.5% on PayPal commercial transactions.
  • Currency conversion: +1% on Stripe when a conversion is required; PayPal builds a 3%–4% spread into the exchange rate instead of a line item.
  • Manually entered cards: +0.5% on Stripe.
  • Disputes: roughly $15 per dispute, and another $15 if you contest it (refunded if you win).

Fees are charged on the amount captured, which usually includes sales tax and shipping. Those dollars pass through your account but are charged the same percentage, so a store with heavy tax and shipping collects less margin than the headline rate suggests.

Pricing so the fee does not eat the target

If you want $40 net from a 2.9% + $0.30 processor, charging $40 leaves you with $38.54. The price that nets $40 is ($40 + $0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.029) = $41.49. The general formula is (target + fixed) ÷ (1 − rate). Recomputing it per product is the only way to keep a target margin intact across price points, because the fixed fee's share changes with every ticket size.

Cards and wallets are also not the only rails. Bank debits are cheaper on large tickets (Stripe ACH 0.8%, capped at $5) and expensive on tiny ones, so the right route depends on your average order value rather than on a single headline percentage.

What this calculator does not include

It does not model monthly platform fees, subscription add-ons, chargeback ratios, refund fee retention, tax remittance, or negotiated volume pricing. It uses the published US rates read in November 2026 from Stripe's pricing page and PayPal's US merchant fee page; your contract, region and payment method can change them. Use it to compare routes and to price a product, then reconcile against a real settlement report.

Frequently asked

How do you calculate a Stripe fee?
Stripe charges a percentage plus a fixed fee per successful charge. For US domestic cards that is 2.9% + $0.30, so a $100 sale costs $3.20 and you keep $96.80. International cards add 1.5%; a required currency conversion adds 1%.
What does PayPal charge per transaction?
PayPal Checkout and Guest Checkout cost 3.49% + $0.49 for US commercial transactions. Card payments taken through an invoice, payment link or Apple Pay cost 2.99% + $0.49. International commercial transactions add 1.50%.
How do I find the price that nets a target amount?
(target + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − percentage rate). To net $100 at 2.9% + $0.30: (100 + 0.30) ÷ 0.971 = $103.30.
Why is my effective rate higher than the advertised rate?
Because the fixed fee does not scale. On a $5 charge, $0.30 is 6% of the sale on its own, so 2.9% + $0.30 becomes 32.9% effective. Raise the average order value or move small tickets to a cheaper rail.
Are fees charged on sales tax and shipping?
Usually yes. Processors charge on the total captured amount. Tax and shipping are not your revenue, but they still cost you the percentage, which is why pre-tax margin can look better than the settlement report.
Which is cheaper, Stripe or PayPal?
On comparable card checkout, Stripe is cheaper per sale at every size in this model: 2.9% + $0.30 against 3.49% + $0.49. PayPal can be cheaper only on the 2.99% + $0.49 card-via-invoice route, which still carries a higher fixed fee.
